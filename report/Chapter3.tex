\label{sec:eda}

An exploratory data analysis was performed to characterize the NERMuD 2023 dataset beyond the basic split counts presented in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. The analysis focuses on three aspects that are relevant for interpreting model performance: the imbalance between domains, the distribution of entity types, and the length of the input sequences.

\subsection{Domain Imbalance}
\label{subsec:eda-domain}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-splits}, the three domains are not equally represented. Wikinews and fiction contain substantially more samples than the ADG domain, both in training and in test data. This imbalance is relevant when interpreting model performance: results on the smaller ADG domain may be influenced by the lower number of training examples, and a model that performs well on WN and FIC is not necessarily equally effective on ADG.

\label{subsec:eda-entities}

The dataset contains three entity types: persons (PER), organizations (ORG), and locations (LOC). Figure~\ref{fig:entity-distribution} presents the number of entities of each type across the splits. Person entities are the most frequent in the training set, followed by locations and organizations, and all three categories are represented in substantial numbers. The distribution is reasonably consistent across splits, ensuring that all entity types are represented in the development and test sets.

\section{Model Training}
\label{sec:train}

Both models were fine-tuned for token-level classification using the Hugging Face \texttt{Transformers} library \cite{wolf2020transformers}. The training procedure was kept as similar as possible between the two models, so that differences in performance can be attributed mainly to the pretrained representations rather than to the training setup.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{subsec:train-config}

The same hyperparameters were used for both models, including the effective batch size. Table~\ref{tab:train-config} summarizes the configuration, and the paragraphs below explain the rationale behind each choice.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Training configuration for UmBERTo and XLM-RoBERTa.}
    \label{tab:train-config}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{UmBERTo} & \textbf{XLM-RoBERTa} \\
        \hline
        Max sequence length & 256 & 256 \\
        Learning rate & $2 \times 10^{-5}$ & $2 \times 10^{-5}$ \\
        Per-device batch size & 8 & 1 \\
        Gradient accumulation & -- & 8 \\
        Effective batch size & 8 & 8 \\
        Epochs & 3 & 3 \\
        Weight decay & 0.01 & 0.01 \\
        Optimizer & AdamW & AdamW \\
        Mixed precision (fp16) & No & Yes \\
        Random seed & 42 & 42 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Maximum sequence length.} A maximum sequence length of 256 tokens was selected based on the exploratory analysis in Section~\ref{subsec:eda-length}, which showed that virtually all sequences in the dataset fall below this limit. This value provides sufficient capacity for the observed sequences while limiting the amount of unnecessary padding during training.

\textbf{Learning rate and weight decay.} A learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ and a weight decay of 0.01 follow common practices for fine-tuning Transformer models on token-classification tasks \cite{devlin2019bert}. Smaller learning rates are typically used for fine-tuning than for training from scratch, because the pretrained weights already encode useful representations that should not be destroyed by large updates.

\textbf{Effective batch size.} Both models were trained with an effective batch size of 8. UmBERTo fits a per-device batch size of 8 directly in GPU memory. XLM-RoBERTa, however, has a much larger vocabulary (250,002 tokens, compared to 32,005 for UmBERTo), which increases the memory required for the embedding layer and the classifier. To keep the effective batch size comparable, XLM-RoBERTa was trained with a per-device batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation over 8 steps. This means that gradients are accumulated over 8 forward passes before each optimizer update, producing an update equivalent to a batch of 8 examples.

\textbf{Mixed precision.} Mixed-precision training (\texttt{fp16}) was enabled for XLM-RoBERTa to reduce memory usage and speed up training on GPU. For UmBERTo, \texttt{fp16} was left disabled, since the model trained comfortably with full precision and the smaller vocabulary did not require the additional memory savings.

\textbf{Number of epochs.} Three epochs were used for both models. This value was chosen as a compromise between underfitting (too few epochs) and overfitting (too many epochs on a relatively small dataset), and the development set was used to verify that no clear overfitting occurred.

\textbf{Optimizer.} The AdamW optimizer, which is the default in the Hugging Face \texttt{Trainer}, was used for both models. AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient update, which generally leads to better generalization than standard Adam.

\textbf{Random seed.} The random seed was fixed to 42 at the beginning of each notebook, controlling weight initialization, data shuffling, and dropout, which improves the reproducibility of the experiments. The \texttt{data\_seed} argument was also set to 42 for XLM-RoBERTa to ensure that data shuffling is reproducible independently of the model seed.

\subsection{Training Dynamics}
\label{subsec:train-dynamics}

The development set was evaluated after every epoch, and the checkpoint with the highest development F1-score was retained for final evaluation. Table~\ref{tab:dev-f1} reports the development F1-score obtained by each model at the end of each epoch.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Development F1-score after each training epoch.}
    \label{tab:dev-f1}
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}
        \hline
        \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Epoch 1} & \textbf{Epoch 2} & \textbf{Epoch 3} \\
        \hline
        UmBERTo & 0.8602 & 0.8724 & \textbf{0.8834} \\
        XLM-RoBERTa & 0.8070 & 0.8359 & \textbf{0.8519} \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Both models improved steadily across the three epochs, and the best development F1-score was obtained in the final epoch in both cases. This suggests that additional epochs could potentially yield further gains, although the improvement between the second and third epochs was already smaller than between the first and the second, indicating that the models were approaching convergence.

Training time differed substantially between the two models. UmBERTo completed three epochs in approximately 18 minutes, whereas XLM-RoBERTa required 1 hour and 11 minutes under the gradient-accumulation and mixed-precision setting described above. This difference reflects both the larger vocabulary of XLM-RoBERTa and the additional forward passes required by gradient accumulation.

The comparison between the two models is fair with respect to the optimization regime: both were trained with the same effective batch size (8), the same learning rate, the same number of epochs, the same data splits, and the same evaluation procedure. Gradient accumulation was used for XLM-RoBERTa so that its effective batch size matches that of UmBERTo, despite the smaller per-device batch size required by its larger vocabulary.

However, the comparison does not control for every possible factor. The two models differ not only in pretraining language coverage but also in tokenizer vocabulary, pretraining corpus, and pretraining objective. Part of the observed difference in performance may therefore be attributable to these factors rather than to the language coverage of the pretraining data alone. The results should be interpreted as a comparison between two specific pretrained checkpoints under a shared training protocol, rather than as a controlled experiment isolating the effect of language-specific pretraining.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:train-impl}

Training was implemented using the Hugging Face \texttt{Trainer} API. The model, tokenizer, data collator, and evaluation metrics were configured through small utility modules (\texttt{config.py}, \texttt{data\_utils.py}, \texttt{metrics.py}, and \texttt{models\_utils.py}) so that both models are trained with the same code path.

Token-level labels were aligned with subword tokens using the standard \texttt{word\_ids} strategy: the first subword of each word receives the original BIO label, while the remaining subwords and special tokens are assigned the label \texttt{-100} and ignored by the loss function \cite{devlin2019bert}. This ensures that the model is trained on word-level entity boundaries rather than on arbitrary subword fragments.

\section{Model Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

Both models were evaluated on the test set using entity-level precision, recall, and F1-score computed with \texttt{seqeval} \cite{ramshaw1999conll,seqeval}. Entity-level metrics are preferred over token-level metrics for NER because they require the complete entity span to be predicted correctly, rather than rewarding partial matches token by token. Token-level accuracy is not reported, since the overwhelming majority of tokens belong to the \texttt{O} class, which makes accuracy a misleading measure of NER performance.

\subsection{Summary of Results}
\label{subsec:eval-summary}

Overall, the results show that UmBERTo outperforms XLM-RoBERTa on Italian NER under the experimental conditions described in this report. The advantage is consistent across entity types and across two of the three domains, and is reflected in all three global metrics. The only exception is the fiction domain, where XLM-RoBERTa obtains a slightly higher F1-score. These results are discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

The results presented in Section~\ref{sec:eval} show that UmBERTo outperforms XLM-RoBERTa on Italian NER under the experimental conditions described in this report, with a global F1-score of 0.8945 against 0.8743. This section interprets these results, discusses the differences between the two models, and identifies the limitations of the study.

\subsection{Language-Specific vs. Multilingual Pretraining}
\label{subsec:disc-pretraining}

The main goal of this project was to investigate whether language-specific pretraining provides an advantage over multilingual pretraining for Italian NER. The results suggest that it does, at least for the NERMuD 2023 dataset and the two checkpoints compared here. UmBERTo, which was pretrained exclusively on Italian text, obtains a higher F1-score than XLM-RoBERTa, which was pretrained on text from 100 languages, in the global evaluation and in two of the three domains.

This result is consistent with the intuition that a model pretrained on a large corpus in the target language can learn language-specific patterns --- morphology, syntax, named-entity conventions --- more effectively than a multilingual model that must allocate capacity across many languages. Italian has particular characteristics that may benefit from dedicated pretraining, such as the relatively rich morphology and the presence of multi-word named entities with prepositions and articles (for example, \textit{Università di Bologna} or \textit{Regione Lombardia}).

However, the advantage of UmBERTo is not uniform across domains, and this nuance is important. The multilingual model obtains a slightly higher F1-score on the fiction domain, which suggests that the benefit of language-specific pretraining is not absolute but depends on the characteristics of the text. Fiction contains a greater lexical and stylistic variety than news or political speeches, and may therefore benefit from the broader pretraining corpus of XLM-RoBERTa. This finding indicates that the choice between language-specific and multilingual models should take into account not only the target language but also the type of text that the model will process.

\subsection{Difficulty by Entity Type}
\label{subsec:disc-entity}

Person entities are the easiest category for both models, with F1-scores above 0.93. This is consistent with the distribution of entity types in the training set, where PER is the most frequent category (9,391 training instances, compared to 6,302 for LOC and 6,122 for ORG). More training examples allow the models to learn the patterns that characterize person names more effectively.

Organizations are the hardest category for both models, with F1-scores between 0.81 and 0.84. This is expected: organization names are often longer, more variable in form, and more ambiguous than person or location names. They may include abbreviations (\textit{ONU}, \textit{UE}), acronyms, nested organization names (\textit{Commissione alleata}), and names that overlap with locations or persons. The annotation of organization spans is therefore more error-prone, both for the annotators who produced the dataset and for the models that must predict them.

Location entities fall between the two extremes, which is also consistent with their position in the training distribution. Location names are typically shorter than organization names and more conventional than person names, but they may still include multi-word expressions and nested structures.

The gap between the two models is consistent across entity types: UmBERTo obtains a higher F1-score than XLM-RoBERTa in every category. This suggests that the advantage of Italian-specific pretraining is not limited to a particular type of entity, but is distributed across the task.

\subsection{Difficulty by Domain}
\label{subsec:disc-domain}

The domain-level results reveal a more nuanced picture than the global scores. UmBERTo performs better on Wikinews and on the ADG domain, but XLM-RoBERTa performs better on fiction. The ADG domain is the hardest for both models, with F1-scores around 0.82. This is consistent with the exploratory analysis in Section~\ref{subsec:eda-domain}: ADG is the smallest domain in the training set (5,147 training instances, compared to 11,423 for FIC and 10,912 for WN) and contains a high proportion of organization entities, which are the hardest entity type for both models.

The advantage of UmBERTo on Wikinews (2.5 F1 points) is the largest single-domain difference. Wikinews contains journalistic text with a relatively standard register and a high density of named entities, which may align well with the Italian corpus used to pretrain UmBERTo. The advantage of XLM-RoBERTa on fiction (0.7 F1 points) is smaller and may reflect the greater lexical and stylistic variety of literary text.

Overall, the domain-level results suggest that the choice between the two models may depend on the target domain. For news and political speeches, UmBERTo appears to be the better choice. For fiction, XLM-RoBERTa obtains a slightly higher F1-score, although the difference is small and may not be statistically significant. A more detailed analysis with multiple random seeds would be necessary to confirm whether this difference is robust.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:disc-limitations}

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

\textbf{Single run per model.} Each model was trained once with a fixed random seed. NER results can vary between runs due to weight initialization, data shuffling, and dropout, and a single run does not allow the variability of the results to be estimated. A more robust comparison would require training each model with multiple seeds and reporting the mean and standard deviation of the F1-score.

\textbf{One checkpoint per model.} The comparison uses a single pretrained checkpoint for each model family. Other checkpoints of the same family (for example, larger versions of XLM-RoBERTa or different UmBERTo variants) might produce different results. The conclusions should therefore be interpreted as applying to the specific checkpoints compared here, rather than to the model families in general.

\textbf{Differences beyond language coverage.} The two models differ not only in pretraining language coverage but also in tokenizer vocabulary, pretraining corpus, and pretraining objective. Part of the observed difference in performance may therefore be attributable to these factors rather than to the language coverage of the pretraining data alone. The results should be interpreted as a comparison between two specific pretrained checkpoints under a shared training protocol, rather than as a controlled experiment isolating the effect of language-specific pretraining.

\textbf{No statistical significance testing.} The differences between the two models were not tested for statistical significance. Given the size of the test set (4,126 examples) and the magnitude of the global difference (2 F1 points), the global difference is likely to be meaningful, but the domain-level differences (in particular the 0.7-point advantage of XLM-RoBERTa on fiction) may not be.

\textbf{Evaluation on a single dataset.} The experiments use the NERMuD 2023 dataset, which covers three domains of Italian text. The results may not generalize to other Italian datasets or to other entity types beyond PER, ORG, and LOC.
